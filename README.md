**Use this when** you are about to write copy for something you sell and the
offer itself has never been written down in one place.

## The rule

> Bad copy is usually a reporting problem. The offer was vague, and the writing
> faithfully reported the vagueness.

Four slots. An offer is not finished until all four are filled by you, not by
me. An empty slot is a result, not a failure.

| slot | the test it has to pass |
|---|---|
| Who it is for | you can name three real ones |
| What changes | before state and after state, in their words |
| What it costs | a number, or an explicit "not set yet" |
| What they do next | one action, and how long it takes |

## How it works

**1. I fill all four slots from what you gave me and label every one.** You
read four labeled lines instead of answering four questions.

**2. I apply the kill test to anything I am about to ask you.** I draft the
offer twice with opposite answers. If the two versions are the same offer, the
question was decoration and I delete it.

**3. I never fill the price slot.** See Limits. A guessed price is the one
error here that costs you money directly.

## The questions that actually move the offer

Not "who is your audience." These:

**Can you name three?** If "who it is for" cannot be three real names,
businesses, or a place you could drive to, it is a category and not an
audience. "Small business owners" fails. "Window cleaners in Utah County with a
van and no website" passes.

**What is the after state, in their sentence?** Not your deliverable. What they
can now say that they could not say last month. "I stopped answering the phone
during dinner" is an after state. "Automated intake" is a deliverable.

**What do they have to believe for this to be worth the price?** That is the
belief gap, and it is what the copy actually has to close. Usually one belief,
not five.

**What is the do nothing option?** Every buyer has one, and it is usually free
and already working badly. Name it. If doing nothing is genuinely fine for
them, you do not have an offer, you have a nice to have.

**What is the cheapest thing that competes?** A cousin, a template, an intern,
a free tool. The offer has to beat that, not beat the premium competitor.

## What you see

```
OFFER SLOTS: the thing you described as "AI consulting"

WHO IT IS FOR
  Service businesses with a van and no website.
  FROM YOU, you listed three past clients who match.
  Passes the name three test.

WHAT CHANGES
  They stop losing calls they never knew came in.
  GUESSED. You described what you build, not what changes for them.
  Load bearing. This is the headline later.

WHAT IT COSTS
  Not set. I will not guess this.
  Two prior projects you mentioned, no price attached to either.

WHAT THEY DO NEXT
  GUESSED, probably a call. You have not said how someone reaches you
  or how long the first conversation runs.

BELIEF GAP
  That a site built in a week is not a worse site.
  GUESSED, but it is the objection in two of the three cases you told me.

DO NOTHING
  Keep running on a Facebook page and word of mouth.
  CHECKED, two of the three businesses you named have no site, only a
  Facebook page with a phone number.

6 answers. 3 guessed, 1 refused on purpose.
Fix the after state and the price, then the copy writes itself.
```

## When a slot stays empty

Say so and stop. Writing copy over an empty price slot produces a page that
cannot ask for the sale, and you will not notice until the page is live and
quiet.

## Limits

I will not set your price. Not a range, not a placeholder, not "most people in
your space charge X." I do not know your costs, your capacity, your market, or
what your last buyer actually paid, and a number I invent becomes the number
you anchor to for a year.

I cannot tell you whether the offer is good. I can only tell you whether it is
specified. A sharply specified offer that nobody wants is still a bad offer,
and finding that out takes selling it, not writing it down.

**The trap:** a naive version of this invents the persona, the transformation,
and the price, and the result reads better than anything you would have written
yourself. That is exactly why it is dangerous. A fluent offer built on three
guesses survives right up until a real buyer reads it, and by then it is on
your site, in your ads, and in your own head as the thing you sell.

## How this one knows what it knows

Every answer it gives itself carries a label: `CHECKED` (it verified it,
and says how), `FROM YOU` (you said it, and it quotes where), or
`GUESSED` (it could not verify). Then it counts the guesses, so you
correct what is wrong instead of trusting confident output built on
silent assumptions.

> Never ask for something you can check.
> Never assume something you cannot check.
